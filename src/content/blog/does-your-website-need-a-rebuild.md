If you've been putting off "sorting the website out" for months, this article is for you. No theory, no design trends, just how to work out what your site actually needs, in the gaps between running your business.

## Start with the honest question

Most owners ask "should we get a new website?" That's the wrong question, because the answer is always yes if you ask someone who sells websites. The better question is: **what specifically is my website failing to do?**

There are only really three answers, and each has a different fix.

## 1. Nobody finds it

If your traffic is tiny, a redesign changes nothing. A beautiful site nobody visits is an expensive business card.

**Check it yourself:** search for what you do plus your town, in a private browser window. Not your business name, the *service*. "Electrician Bolton". "Accountant Chester". If you're nowhere in the first two pages and not in the map results, visibility is your problem, not design.

**The fix is usually:** completing your Google Business Profile properly, and adding genuinely useful pages for the services and towns you serve. That's local SEO work, not a rebuild.

## 2. People find it and leave

This is the most common one, and the most fixable.

**Check it yourself:** open your site on your phone, on mobile data, not office wifi. Count the seconds until you can read something. Over three, and you're losing a meaningful share of visitors before they see anything at all.

Then look at what's on that first screen. Can a stranger tell what you do, where you are, and how to contact you, without scrolling? If not, that's the problem.

**The fix is usually:** compressing images, removing the slideshow, putting a tappable phone number in the header, and rewriting the first paragraph. None of that needs a rebuild.

## 3. People enquire, but they're the wrong people

Price shoppers ringing about jobs you don't want, enquiries from businesses far too small to serve profitably, questions you've answered on the site three times.

**The fix is usually:** being more specific. Say who you work with best. Publish indicative prices or ranges. Describe what a typical job actually involves. This loses you enquiries you were declining anyway.

## So when *is* a rebuild justified?

Genuinely, when one of these is true:

- **You can't update it.** The person who built it is gone, or every change is a paid ticket. That cost compounds every year.
- **It isn't responsive.** Built before about 2015, so it doesn't adapt to a phone at all. Retrofitting is usually more expensive than rebuilding.
- **It's a security liability.** An abandoned platform with out-of-date plugins is a problem waiting to happen.
- **The business has changed.** You do different work, for different customers, than the site describes.
- **The structure is wrong.** You need twelve service pages and it has one, and there's nowhere sensible to put them.

Notice that "it looks dated" isn't on the list by itself. Looking dated matters, but a tidy-up often fixes it for a fraction of the cost.

## Do this before you spend anything

1. **Run PageSpeed Insights** on your homepage. Free, from Google, takes thirty seconds. Note the mobile score.
2. **Check Search Console** if you have it, or just search as described above. Are you found for services, or only your name?
3. **Ask three customers** how they found you and what they thought of the site. You'll learn more in three conversations than from any audit.
4. **List what you actually want it to do.** More enquiries? Better ones? Online bookings? Fewer phone calls asking your opening hours?

With those four things in hand, you can have a sensible conversation with anyone, and you'll know immediately whether they're listening or reciting a sales script.

## The honest summary

Most small business websites don't need rebuilding. They need to load faster, say what the business does in the first ten seconds, and make contact obvious. That's a fortnight's work, not a five-figure project.

The ones that genuinely do need rebuilding usually have a structural problem you can name in one sentence. If you can't name it, get a second opinion before you spend.
